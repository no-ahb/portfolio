How this version relates to the installation

The original engine assigns material to fourteen discrete speakers, combines three playback speeds, and measures its own output in six frequency bands. This browser instrument uses up to six overlapping voices, gentle fades, and stereo panning. Filtered random motion searches the map; measured brightness nudges the next selection toward a different spectral region. Holding a position keeps the same material in circulation.

The spatial view shows timbral coordinates, not speaker positions. It uses the available listening excerpts and omits the original engine's pre-composed sketches, long-form density cycles, and fourteen-channel routing. The finished composition includes Noah's subsequent editing and selection.

In September 2025, I recorded a string quintet playing through an “orchestra of objects” (cymbals, gongs, piano frames, sheets of metal and paper, all with transducers attached). We used extended-range microphones that record above the limit of human hearing and recorded at ultrahigh sample rates (192 kHz).

This generated an enormous amount of material, so I decided to try composing a 14-channel piece by analyzing and mapping the recordings, then writing a program to traverse the map in real time. This resulted in the 35-minute piece ANTIPHON, shown at the Haus für Medienkunst from March through May 2026.

Part I: Recording

Before the musicians arrived, we had a single evening to set up at Studio Richter Mahr. Chris Kalcov, the engineer, masterfully organized a system for recording and re-amping 32 channels on a Neve 5088 (RME Horus / MADI) at 192 kHz, operating at the very limit of what the equipment and the room could manage.

We – a bassist, cellist, violist, and two violinists, including myself – ended up recording 10 hours of audio over two days. Chris re-amped the recordings overnight through the objects and re-recorded the result, so we ended up with about 20 hours of audio.

More technical details: the instruments were close-miked with extended-HF Schoeps capsules and Coles 4038 ribbons; the bass with a Sennheiser MKH 800; the room with a Schoeps pair, a DPA 4006A, and an AEA R84 ribbon; and the objects with Schoeps, a Neumann M49, and a Sennheiser MKH 20 on the bass drum. At 192 kHz, the files (ideally!) reach 96 kHz. The extended-HF capsules carry real content to roughly 40–80 kHz, which we were able to verify with spectrograms and pitch shifting.

The instructions for the session were broad and open, loosely structuring pitch and rhythmic frameworks while relying heavily on the improvisatory intuition of the musicians.

Part II: Corpus

The corpus consisted of 1,615 files, many of them as long as 45 minutes. The material I cared most about was ultrasonic, which meant slowing the audio to quarter speed to bring it into the audible range, quadrupling its duration.

Trying to build systematically, take by take, quickly became overwhelming. So instead, I turned to a process-based approach. First, I organized the corpus by timbre and mapped it. Then I created a program to traverse that mapped, multidimensional space. I thought this would be an elegant way to draw out interrelationships within the material that weren’t immediately obvious.

To make it work for 14 speakers, I chose to think of each speaker as a single “voice,” clustered near other speakers by timbre but sounding a distinct take or microphone perspective. You might expect the brass sheet to be timbrally very similar to the same moment in time through the steel sheet, such that Speaker 1 might play back the recording of the brass while Speaker 2 plays the same instant through the steel. But perhaps a totally different moment in time turns out to be timbrally similar to another – or one material is unexpectedly similar to another (e.g., paper and a bass drum) – and the corpus traversal begins to bridge and fracture through the material in more interesting, web-like ways.

Part III: Analysis

To analyze the material, I sliced the corpus into 303,799 five-second windows with a 2.5-second hop and extracted 34 descriptive features per window: spectral centroid, flatness, flux, contrast, and rolloff; pitch and a custom pitch-confidence measure; 13 MFCCs; a 20–75 Hz “roughness” measure; and an ultrasonic-energy ratio.

To make that 34-dimensional space walkable, I used UMAP and colored the result by recording layer, roughness, or ultrasonic content. The visual representation of the sessions is below – a vaporous sort of splash.

Part IV: Playback

I built a lightweight browser-based exploration tool to begin listening through different segments of the corpus and becoming familiar with it. Click any point and it streams that five-second window back. Select a region to explore its timbral character. The corpus becomes something you explore by ear.

Finally, I wrote a generative engine to play it back in SuperCollider, specific to the fourteen-speaker room at the Haus für Medienkunst.

A cursor drifts across the map by filtered Brownian motion, with a drift speed (from calm to storm), a range (stay local or roam), path smoothness, and a “gravity” that pulls toward dense regions; every step pulls new five-second windows to play across the speakers. Each window can play at three speeds: 1x, 0.5x, or 0.25x. At full speed, it’s recognizable instruments and music; at half speed, it becomes more textural and uncanny; at quarter speed, it becomes grain, ultrasonics, hiss, and the sound of the recording chain.

The engine listens back to itself for some awareness of its state: a small analysis synth taps its own output, splits it into six bands, and reports the spectral centroid and spread. That (ideally) drives a homeostasis loop so the field never collapses or fully stagnates.

In the end, I found the map too unpredictable to trust for an unattended two-month run. It had long stretches of uninspired wandering interspersed with flashes of musical genius. So I recorded long passes of the engine running, listened for about two days, curated the 5–8-minute chunks I particularly liked, and reassembled them in Reaper. The result is a hybrid of machine and human composition. Listen to the finished recording.
