Approach

In September 2025, I recorded a string quintet playing through an “orchestra of objects” using microphones that could capture sounds far above the limit of human hearing.

We recorded for two days at 192 kHz, generating an enormous amount of material. Rather than compose directly from the recordings, I decided to organize them by timbre and build a program to explore the relationships between them.

The result was ANTIPHON, a 33-minute, 20-second piece exhibited at the Haus für Medienkunst from March through May 2026.

Recording

Before the musicians arrived, Chris Kalcov, the engineer, masterfully organized a system for recording and re-amping 32 channels at 192 kHz, at the very limit of what the equipment and the room could handle.

We – a bassist, cellist, violist, and two violinists, including myself – recorded for 10 hours over two days. Microphones captured the instruments directly, the room, and an assortment of resonating objects, including sheets of metal, wood, brass, copper, gongs, and drums.

Much of the material I was interested in was ultrasonic, beyond the range of human hearing. Slowing the recordings to quarter speed brought these frequencies into the audible range, revealing sounds that were otherwise inaccessible.

Corpus

The final collection contained 1,615 files, some as long as 45 minutes. I was also slowing many of them to a fraction of their original speed: at quarter speed, a 45-minute recording became three hours. Trying to compose systematically, take by take, quickly became overwhelming.

Instead, I decided on a process-based approach. I divided the recordings into 303,799 overlapping five-second windows and analyzed each for 34 features describing pitch, texture, and spectral character. I then used UMAP to arrange these windows into a map, placing sounds with similar characteristics near one another.

I liked the idea of discovering relationships that weren’t immediately obvious. Two different materials might sound similar at the same moment, but so might two completely unrelated recordings. I imagined each speaker as a separate vantage onto the same body of sound. One might linger with a brass sheet, another veer toward paper or bass drum, another catch a related texture from an entirely different moment. The room became a way of hearing the corpus from multiple perspectives at once, rather than following the original recordings in time.

Playback

I first built a browser tool to explore the recordings by clicking around the map. You can try a simplified version above, containing a fraction of the original corpus.

I then built a generative playback engine in SuperCollider for the fourteen-speaker room.

A cursor wanders through the map, selecting new five-second recordings to play across the speakers. I could control how quickly it moved, how far it wandered, and how closely it followed regions of similar sound.

Each recording could play at full, half, or quarter speed. At full speed, the original instruments remain recognizable. Slowed down, they become increasingly strange: textures, grain, hiss, and sounds from the recording equipment itself.

Composing

Initially, I hoped the system could run autonomously for the entire exhibition. But I found it too unpredictable to fully trust. It produced long stretches of uninspired wandering interspersed with flashes of genius.

So I recorded long passes of the program running, spent about two days listening through them, and selected the five- to eight-minute passages I liked most. I then assembled these in Reaper to form the final composition.

The result is a hybrid of machine and human composition.
