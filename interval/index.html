The string closes the circuit.

The pickup measures vibration; a driver returns energy to the string. The module has to hold that electromagnetic relationship steady while still allowing the string's tension to be adjusted.

Seat the driver in the module

The CAD design includes a Ø20 mm recess under the string. Seating the round coil in the hardwood fixes its relationship to the string. The close-up photographs below show the built arrangement.

Develop the mounting geometry

The archive preserves a version-four wooden-prototype drawing alongside STEP and STL models. Tuning hardware, coil seating, string anchoring, and the jack connection were brought into one fabricated module.

Credit the circuits, own the integration

The electronics integrate Brian Thornock's Stealth sustainer and the open-source Thunder Boost. My work includes the instrument's CAD and construction, coil winding and potting, the per-string chain, and tuning the five installed modules to the space.

INTERVAL is five steel strings on a gallery wall, each sounding continuously on its own — no player, no hammer. Each string is held in self-oscillation by an electromagnetic sustainer. I designed and built the string modules and the mechanical instrument, assembled the sustainer electronics from open-source designs, and tuned each string so its pitch is set by the architecture of the room. It’s the self-oscillating sibling of Suspension and Setting (N1 5SH).

How a string sounds itself

A pickup senses the steel string’s vibration, and an electromagnetic driver returns energy to it. With suitable gain and phase, the feedback sustains the string’s motion. Length and tension set its resonant frequencies; the electronics keep supplying energy.

What I built, and what I integrated

I integrated Brian Thornock’s open-source Stealth sustainer and the open-source “Thunder Boost”, whose boards I had fabricated at Aisler. My electronics work involved winding and potting the driver coils, building the per-string signal chain, and getting five modules to operate together. The source circuits are credited to their designers.

The original design is the instrument itself. I designed each string module in CAD: a hardwood block with a machine head for tuning, a recessed Ø20 mm bore that seats the round driver coil directly under the string, a string anchor at the far end, and a ¼-inch jack output. It went through four hand-built wooden prototypes before the CAD version.

Tuning to the room

The strings aren’t tuned to musical notes. Each module’s machine head sets length and tension so the string resonates at a frequency chosen for the space — the pitches come from the architecture and materials of the gallery. Because the modules are bolted to the wall, the wall itself radiates the sound.

Result

Five strings, five continuous tones, tuned to the building — shown as As Long as a Piece of String at the Sarabande Foundation, London, 2024.
