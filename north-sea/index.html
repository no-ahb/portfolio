Approach

NORTH SEA began with a simple question: what does the movement of the sea sound like?

Rather than make music inspired by the sea (a storied lineage, from Debussy’s La Mer and Mendelssohn’s Hebrides Overture to Éliane Radigue’s Occam Ocean), I wanted to let the sea play an instrument directly. I liked the idea of applying musical processes like recording, transposition, and performance to the movement of the sea itself. So I built a system to record the movement of a buoy in the North Sea and reproduce that movement through hundreds of piano strings.

My buoy

I built a buoy with a Raspberry Pi Pico 2 and a motion sensor to record acceleration and orientation.

The buoy (pronounced “boy” by the Brits) became a running joke of sorts with my flatmates: “How’s your boy today?” The joke acquired a particular poignance when I lost him at sea in a powerful storm in late November.

Buoy, sensor, and simultaneous acceleration and orientation recording.

Surge, sway and heave

The buoy records acceleration, but the strings need position. So I processed the recorded data to reconstruct the buoy’s movement along three axes: surge, sway, and heave.

Each acceleration sample is rotated out of the sensor's frame and into the world frame using that sample's quaternion.

A high-pass filter removes slow drift; a low-pass filter removes rapid noise.

The result is then decimated from 50 Hz to 10 Hz, averaging to eliminate noise.

Playing the strings

A second microcontroller reads this recording and drives three motorized carriages, reproducing the buoy’s movement by plucking hundreds of piano strings. I designed a custom controller board and wrote software to smoothly interpolate between the recorded positions.

Building the instrument

I looked to the construction of pianos and harps, using inexpensive industrial materials to build a wall-mounted, transportable frame capable of withstanding the considerable tension (20+ tons/frame) of hundreds of strings.

I developed a parametric tool to design the steel plates, had them laser-cut, then assembled and strung the instrument by hand.

Result

The installation continuously replays the original 25-hour recording of the North Sea as a piece of durational music.
