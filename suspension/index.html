Nine suspended steel wires, sustained by electromagnetic feedback.

Approach

At the center of SUSPENSION is a steel piano wire tensioned by my body weight in lead. Eight other wires hang around it, tuned such that their overtones alternately meet or interfere with one another.

To allow the strings to sound and sustain while continuously changing, I built an electromagnetic feedback system that listens and modulates the strings' behavior in real time.

Feedback

First, a magnetic pickup detects the wire's vibrations. The signal passes through a series of slowly moving filters, which select different harmonics, before returning to an electromagnetic driver that keeps the wire moving.

Once set in motion, the wire can sustain itself through this feedback, shifting between different pitches and textures as the filters move.

Hardware

Preamp

The pickup's signal is tiny, so it requires a preamp to amplify it without introducing noise or placing much load on the coil.

Daisy Seed

The Daisy Seed listens to the pickup and decides which harmonics to encourage.

  1. ListenFollow pitch and energy levels across frequency bands
  2. SelectFollow three harmonics
  3. ReturnLimit the level; drive the wire

Three filters slowly wander through the spectrum, settling on growing resonances before moving away to let others emerge. Automatic gain control prevents the feedback from running away.

The speakers in the room receive the unfiltered pickup signal, so what you hear is the wire itself.

Power amplifier and driver

The Daisy's output is too weak to move the wire directly. So I built a separate amplifier board to drive a custom-wound electromagnet.

A permanent magnet provides a steady magnetic field. Current flowing through the coil changes this field, exerting a changing force on the nearby steel wire. With the right timing, each cycle adds a little energy to the vibration, keeping it sounding.

Related work

Related precedents include Andrew McPherson's magnetic resonator piano and the electromagnetically prepared piano developed by Per Bloland, Steven Backer, and Edgar Berdahl at CCRMA in 2005–06.

Tuning

A vibrating string produces many pitches at once. It moves along its whole length, but also in halves, thirds, quarters, and so on. These higher patterns produce a series of pitches called harmonics.

I tuned the nine strings using simple whole-number relationships, so that harmonics from different strings could meet at precisely the same frequency. For example, the thirteenth harmonic of one string was designed to meet the sixth of the central string at 294 Hz, whereas the other harmonics of each string are at different frequencies. The room's tuning is represented by the image below.

On paper, these relationships are exact. In practice, the wires are constantly changing -- and humans are imperfect at measuring and mounting strings. Temperature, settling, and tiny changes in tension all affect their pitch. The physical instrument only ever approaches the ideal.

Beating

When two pitches are close but not quite identical, they interfere, causing the sound of pulses. This is called beating.

For example, two strings tuned to 196 and 196.4 Hz produce a pulse every 2.5 seconds. Tuned closer together, the pulse slows. Further apart, it speeds up, becoming a flutter, then roughness, and eventually two distinct pitches.

As the feedback filters wander between harmonics, these relationships continually shift. Sometimes the strings seem to merge into one sound; at other times, they pull apart.

Documentation

The system ran continuously throughout the two-month exhibition. With no one in the room, the building joined the instrument: creaks, clicks, and the rain outside entered the field of feedback and harmonics.
