Approach

I built this system for live violin and electronics performance, allowing me to layer sounds without fixed rhythms or predictable repetitions.

The idea came from Pauline Oliveros’s Expanded Instrument System, a network of delays and signal processing designed for improvisation. Oliveros imagined it as a kind of time machine, allowing performers to hear past and present sounds together. The system belongs to a longer tradition of tape-delay experimentation, including the work of Terry Riley, Brian Eno, and Robert Fripp.

My system combines stacked delay lines with 24 independent, variable-length loops, distributed across multiple speakers. It also includes distortion, octave shifting, reverse playback, and granular freeze effects, all controlled by foot pedals.

How it works

The delay network routes sound between multiple speakers, with feedback levels and connections that slowly change over time.

Each new loop is assigned a random duration between 10 and 60 seconds. Loops drift independently across the speakers and fade out over time, making room for new recordings.

The interface shows each loop’s position, duration, and feedback in real time, providing a simple visual overview of an otherwise complex system.

Next steps

I’d like to run the system on a Bela or Raspberry Pi, replacing the laptop and screen with dedicated hardware and simple LED indicators.
